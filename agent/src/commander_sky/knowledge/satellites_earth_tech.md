## What satellites are and how they stay up
- A satellite is anything that orbits something else — the Moon is Earth's natural satellite; everything we've launched is an artificial one.
- A satellite stays up by falling: it moves sideways so fast (about 28,000 km/h in low orbit) that as gravity pulls it down, the Earth's surface curves away beneath it — it is perpetually falling around the planet, never into it.
- There's no engine running to keep a satellite up. Once it's in orbit, it coasts — the same physics that has kept the Moon circling Earth for billions of years.
- Low-orbit satellites still skim the very top of the atmosphere, so they slowly lose speed and altitude. The International Space Station needs regular engine "reboosts" or it would eventually fall back to Earth.
- Sputnik 1, launched by the Soviet Union on October 4, 1957, was the first artificial satellite — a polished metal sphere about the size of a beach ball that beeped on the radio for three weeks and started the Space Age.
- Vanguard 1, launched by the United States in 1958, is the oldest human-made object still in orbit — a grapefruit-sized satellite that stopped transmitting long ago but keeps silently circling.

## Orbits — low, high, and geostationary
- Low Earth orbit (LEO) is roughly 200 to 2,000 km up — home of the International Space Station (about 400 km), Hubble, and most satellites ever launched. One lap around Earth takes about 90 minutes.
- Geostationary orbit is special: at 35,786 km above the equator, one orbit takes exactly one day, so the satellite hovers over the same spot on Earth. That's why a satellite TV dish can point at one fixed place in the sky.
- The science-fiction writer Arthur C. Clarke described the geostationary communications relay idea in 1945 — twelve years before the first satellite ever flew.
- In between sits medium Earth orbit, where navigation satellites like GPS live, about 20,000 km up, circling Earth twice a day.
- Polar orbits pass over both poles while the Earth turns underneath, letting one satellite photograph the entire planet strip by strip — ideal for weather and mapping.

## Satellites you use every day
- GPS is a constellation of around 30 satellites carrying atomic clocks. Your phone finds itself by comparing tiny differences in the arrival time of their signals from at least four satellites.
- The clocks on GPS satellites run measurably differently than clocks on the ground — exactly as Einstein's relativity predicts — and the system must correct for it constantly or positions would drift by kilometers every day.
- Weather forecasts depend on satellites: geostationary sentinels like the GOES series stare at the same hemisphere around the clock, watching hurricanes form and move — storm warnings days in advance are a satellite gift.
- Communications satellites relay TV, phone calls, and internet across oceans; ships and airliners far from any cell tower stay connected through them.
- The Landsat program has photographed the entire Earth continuously since 1972 — the longest continuous record of our planet from space, tracking forests, glaciers, cities, and coastlines across five decades.
- Mega-constellations changed the game in the 2020s: SpaceX's Starlink alone has thousands of small satellites in low orbit (several thousand active as of mid-2026) beaming internet to places cables never reached. Astronomers work with operators to keep their reflections from spoiling telescope images.

## Space telescopes — satellites that look outward
- The Hubble Space Telescope, launched in 1990, orbits about 500 km up — above the shimmering atmosphere that blurs ground-based views, which is why its pictures are so sharp.
- The James Webb Space Telescope doesn't orbit Earth at all: it sits about 1.5 million km away at a gravitational balance point called L2, where it keeps its giant sunshield between its instruments and the Sun.
- Plenty of "satellites" point down instead of up — and some do both: the same orbital perch works for studying Earth or the universe.

## Space junk and keeping orbit clean
- Tens of thousands of pieces of debris large enough to track — dead satellites, spent rocket stages, fragments from collisions — are circling Earth right now, alongside millions of smaller bits.
- Orbital debris travels around 28,000 km/h, so even a paint fleck can chip a spacecraft window; the ISS occasionally fires thrusters to dodge tracked debris.
- The nightmare scenario is called the Kessler syndrome: collisions creating debris that causes more collisions, in a cascade that could make some orbits unusable — proposed by NASA scientist Donald Kessler in 1978.
- The fix is good housekeeping: modern satellites are designed to deorbit and burn up in the atmosphere at end of life, and low-orbit satellites like Starlink's are low enough that atmospheric drag cleans them up within years, not centuries.
