## Hubble Space Telescope
- Launched in 1990 and still observing after more than 35 years — serviced five times by Space Shuttle astronauts, the only telescope ever repaired in orbit.
- Its first images were blurry: the main mirror was ground to the wrong shape by about 1/50th the width of a human hair. Astronauts installed corrective optics in 1993 — effectively giving Hubble glasses.
- The Hubble Deep Field pointed at a "blank" patch of sky the size of a pinhead at arm's length for ten days — and found about 3,000 galaxies in it. Deep fields since then show the universe is stuffed with galaxies in every direction.
- Hubble's measurements of exploding stars helped discover that the universe's expansion is speeding up — evidence for dark energy, and a Nobel Prize.

## James Webb Space Telescope
- Launched Christmas Day 2021; its 6.5-meter gold-coated mirror unfolded in space from 18 hexagonal segments — origami engineering with zero room for error.
- Webb sees infrared light, so it needs to be extremely cold: a tennis-court-sized five-layer sunshield keeps its instruments below −220°C.
- It orbits the Sun at L2, about 1.5 million km from Earth — four times farther than the Moon, too far for any repair mission.
- Webb sees "cosmic dawn": galaxies from a few hundred million years after the Big Bang, some surprisingly big and bright — a genuine puzzle that has theorists rethinking early galaxy growth.
- It also reads the atmospheres of planets around other stars, detecting molecules like water vapor and carbon dioxide in starlight filtered through alien skies.

## Exoplanets — planets around other stars
- The first planet found orbiting a Sun-like star, 51 Pegasi b (1995), earned a Nobel Prize; more than 5,500 exoplanets are confirmed as of mid-2026.
- The workhorse trick is the transit method: watch a star long enough and a planet crossing its face dims it by a tiny, repeating amount. Kepler stared at 150,000 stars at once and found thousands this way.
- The variety is wild: "hot Jupiters" that orbit in days, worlds where it may rain molten glass, planets orbiting two stars at once, and rocky planets in the "habitable zone" where liquid water is possible.
- The TRAPPIST-1 system packs seven roughly Earth-sized planets around one small red star — several in its habitable zone; Webb has been checking whether they have atmospheres at all.
- Statistically, our galaxy holds more planets than stars — hundreds of billions of worlds.

## Hearing the universe — gravitational waves
- In 2015 the LIGO detectors felt space itself stretch and squeeze as two black holes collided over a billion light-years away — the first gravitational wave detection, confirming Einstein's century-old prediction. Nobel Prize, 2017.
- The instruments measure length changes thousands of times smaller than a proton across 4-km arms — arguably the most precise measurement humans have ever made.
- In 2017 detectors and telescopes together watched two neutron stars collide — the blast forged heavy elements, showing where much of the universe's gold and platinum comes from.

## Seeing black holes
- The Event Horizon Telescope — a network of radio dishes spanning the whole planet — produced the first image of a black hole's shadow in 2019 (galaxy M87), then imaged Sagittarius A*, the black hole at the center of our own Milky Way, in 2022.
- Black holes aren't cosmic vacuum cleaners: orbit at a safe distance and they behave like any other mass. The danger is only up close, where not even light escapes.
